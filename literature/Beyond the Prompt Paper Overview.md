\n**Beyond the Prompt: Deploying Medical Foundation Models on Diverse Chest X-ray Populations**

Foundation models (FMs) in medical image analysis have shown major improvements in performance over the years. But across diverse patient populations, such as adult and pediatric cases, this remains challenging. This study evaluates different approaches for deploying FMs in clinical scenarios across diverse patient populations. It explores the importance of text prompt design in terms of zero-shot (ZS) performance. While most medical FMs trained extensively on web-based datasets have demonstrated significant potential to advance healthcare applications, their deployment to clinical settings remains challenging because of the severe consequences of incorrect diagnoses. Additionally, for out-of-distribution data, they experience performance drops, such as when transitioning from adult to pediatric cases. ZS performance from FMs depends not only on the image but also on the given text prompt, since many FMs are trained to derive predictions from vision-language similarities. Hence, determining the optimal text prompt to achieve the best ZS performance on newer distributions still poses a major challenge. The most common strategy is to adapt and fine-tune, but this requires a great deal of computational resources, time, and effort. This study focuses on a state-of-the-art (SOTA), open-source, and lightweight approach to identify effective strategies for the successful clinical application of FMs. For the FM, this study uses MedImageInsight, which was trained on adult data, to examine the prediction of pneumonia in adult (in-domain) and pediatric (out-of-domain) cases using training-free ZS and training-based approaches. The key contributions are:

1. ZS ensemble for training-free FM deployment
2. Analysis of FM adaptation trade-offs
3. Multi-site evaluation
4. Bias assessment

This study proposes a method that evaluates MedImageInsight for pneumonia detection across three different domains, using nine different prompt types and enhanced prompt and vision ensembles, as well as exploring training-based methods. MedImageInsight contains three components: an image encoder, a text encoder, and a text decoder. The architecture works as follows: templates, along with radiology reports, are fed into the text encoder for each class. Since there are two classes, a number of prompt combinations for each class are averaged to become t0 and t1. The image is then augmented into B views, where B = 64 in this case. Each augmented image goes into the image encoder and is converted into embeddings. These embeddings then go into the confidence score filter, which uses the text embeddings as a guide to calculate the entropy of the image embeddings. Entropy generally defines randomness, meaning a 50-50 prediction, which should not be used. Here, only the 10% of images with the lowest entropy are taken and then averaged. The averaged embedding is then compared with the text embeddings using cosine similarity; whichever text embedding class has the higher cosine similarity is the class assigned by the classifier.

Different forms of evaluation were performed. For template-as-prompt, among five types of prompt templates, the "chest+{class}" prompt template showed the highest accuracy on both the adult and pediatric datasets. Next, for radiology reports as prompts, MIMIC performed best, followed by CheXpert, using a part of the report. When using the full radiology report, performance dropped by 7% in accuracy. Prompt ensembles further improved ZS performance. For the adult dataset, using templates plus reports was most effective, whereas for the pediatric dataset, template ensembles demonstrated significant improvement, with a 7% increase in TPR. In combined prompt and vision ensembles, accuracy improved for the adult dataset, but TPR slightly dropped for the pediatric dataset. This describes the zero-shot method.

For the training-based adaptation method, the authors tried multiple approaches. One is lightweight adapter training, where the image encoder remains frozen and linear probing is applied along with kNN (k=5). The next approach is full fine-tuning using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), where the image encoder's parameters are adapted using low-intrinsic-rank updates (rank r=8, selected empirically). Baseline models used for comparison included DenseNet-121, CheXagent, RAD-DINO, and BiomedCLIP.

The results from using prompt ensembles show highly valuable and improved performance in ZS settings. Performance ranked as follows: highest on CheXpert, then MIMIC, and finally the pediatric dataset VinDr-PCXR. The results show that when the chest X-ray image comes from a distribution similar to the training data, prompt ensembles (including radiology reports) enhance ZS performance. Vision ensembles further improved performance as more variability was added. However, for the VinDr-PCXR pediatric dataset, radiology reports hurt performance, mainly because pediatric anatomy, clinical terms, and norms/findings do not match adult reports, since most FMs are pretrained on adult reports. Instead, template ensembles alone worked best for the pediatric dataset. Vision ensembles also did not work well on the pediatric dataset, as the augmented views failed to align with the known distribution. Further checks using BiomedCLIP confirmed that vision ensembles are effective on adult datasets but performed consistently poorly on the pediatric dataset across all ZS scenarios. These results refer to the ZS ensemble method, which dominates in low-data regimes. For increased training data (i.e., adult data), training-based methods are useful. Full fine-tuning demonstrated only marginal gains over ZS ensembling, whereas linear probing required more than 10% and 50% of the data on MIMIC and CheXpert, respectively, to outperform ZS ensembling. MedImageInsight shows great performance on the in-domain adult dataset. For the out-of-domain dataset, ZS baselines failed, achieving a TPR below 50%. DenseNet's performance was also unsatisfactory, requiring more than 80% of labeled data just to achieve a TPR above 50%. LoRA fine-tuning demonstrated the best performance among all methods, using just 50% of the training data. In the bias assessment, LoRA and kNN exhibited the smallest differences across all variables, including sex, race, and age.

These findings demonstrate the potential of ZS ensembles as a resource-efficient alternative to training-based adaptation methods, especially in low-data and low-compute settings. In scenarios where data is sufficient, training-based methods such as linear probing and LoRA work best.
