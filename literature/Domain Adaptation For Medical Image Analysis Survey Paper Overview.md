\nMachine Learning is widely used in medical image analysis. However, during testing, the data is assumed to have a similar data distribution to the training data. Previous studies have shown that the test error generally increases in proportion to the distribution difference between the training and testing datasets, and this problem is known as domain shift. In medical image analysis, labeled data is generally expensive and time-consuming to obtain, and also requires labor-intensive participation by experts. In order to solve the domain shift and lack of labeled data issues, there have been a number of studies using domain adaptation. The paper categorizes existing methods into two types: shallow models and deep models, which are further divided into supervised, semi-supervised, and unsupervised methods.

Domain shift is a very common problem faced during the practical application of various ML algorithms and may cause significant performance degradation. A study done on MRI data from four independent sites found changes in image-level distribution caused by different scanners. The DA methods for medical image analysis can be classified into five problem settings, i.e., model type, label availability, modality difference, number of sources, and adaptation steps.

1. Model Type:It includes shallow DA and deep DA, where shallow DA methods usually rely on human-engineered image features with conventional ML models, whereas deep DA methods usually rely on deep neural networks (particularly CNNs) to generate features and train in an end-to-end pipeline.
    
2. Label Availability:In supervised DA, labeled data for both the source and target is available. In semi-supervised DA, labeled data is available only for the source domain. Whereas in unsupervised DA, both the source and target domains work with unlabeled data.
    
3. Modality Difference: In single-modality DA, similar data-capturing techniques are used (e.g., the source has MRI images from vendor A and the target has MRI images from vendor B). In multi-modality DA, data in the source and target domains are collected using different imaging techniques (e.g., the source has MR images, whereas the target domain has CT images).
    
4. Number of Sources: The model is trained on a single source in single-source DA, whereas it is trained on multiple sources in multi-source DA, which is comparatively more challenging.
    
5. Adaptation Step: If the adaptation process follows a single step from the source to the target domain, then it is called single-step DA. However, if it follows intermediate domains to gradually reduce the distribution gap between the source and target, then it is called multi-step DA.
    

Shallow DA methods follow two commonly used strategies, i.e., instance weighting and feature transformation. In instance weighting, the images in the source domain are assigned different weights according to their relevance to the target domain, so images that are more relevant are assigned larger weights. A study using instance weighting-based DA for Alzheimer's disease classification showed better results than methods that only used data from either the source or target domain. Feature transformation focuses on mapping source and target samples from their original feature spaces to a new shared feature representation space. It uses various techniques, such as low-rank representation, to construct a shared feature space and reduce the distribution gap. A study using a feature transformation-based DA method for microscopy image analysis trained a boosting classifier on data in the transformed common space and achieved good performance. In the case of semi-supervised shallow DA, a study used PCA to transform the source and target domains into a common space that minimized the domain difference and then trained a random forest classifier on that transformed source domain, before fine-tuning it with a few labeled target samples to further reduce domain shift. A study based on an unsupervised shallow DA method used instance weighting for lung disease classification. Gaussian texture features (a type of image feature where a central pixel is assigned high weight, with smoothly reducing weights that mimic a Gaussian distribution) were extracted from images, and then a weighted logistic classifier was trained. This reduced the domain shift and significantly improved the classification performance.

Deep DA methods are mainly dominated by CNN architectures. They have demonstrated superiority over conventional human-engineered imaging features. After AlexNet, some CNN-based architectures that are widely used in various DA models for medical image analysis are VGG, Inception, ResNet, and DenseNet. In supervised deep DA, a study used ResNet as the feature extractor for mammographic images. Using those features, they evaluated multiple shallow DA methods for breast cancer classification. Another study proposed first pretraining an AlexNet-like network on ImageNet and then fine-tuning it with ROIs for breast cancer classification. Another study proposed pretraining a VGG network on ImageNet and then using labeled MRI data to fine-tune it for Alzheimer's disease classification. They selected the most informative training samples based on entropy. Some studies faced an issue where, due to fewer target samples, they could not fine-tune the model and hence leaned towards multi-step DA. One experiment followed this method and achieved better results than directly transferring the model. A study proposed a semi-supervised DA method for electron microscopy image segmentation, where one encoder and two decoders were used. One decoder was used for segmentation, whereas the other was used for the reconstruction of images from both the source and target domains. After training, the reconstruction decoder was discarded, and the whole model was fine-tuned with labeled target samples to obtain a domain-adapted segmentation model. Another study proposed a GAN-based DA framework for chest X-ray image classification. Here, the discriminator performed three-category classification (normal, disease, and generated image). When experimented on the NIH dataset, it demonstrated its effectiveness. Unsupervised deep DA methods have different strategies, including feature alignment, image alignment, image + feature alignment, ensemble learning, soft labels, and feature learning. Feature alignment focuses on learning domain-invariant features across domains through specially designed CNN models. A study proposed a DANN-based multi-connected adversarial network for brain lesion segmentation. In this architecture, the segmentation network, along with a domain discriminator, is trained simultaneously. Experiments on the DRIVE dataset demonstrated its effectiveness. Another study proposed a cross-modality DA framework for cardiac MR and CT image segmentation by freezing the higher-level layers and only adapting the lower-level layers to reduce domain shift during training.Because they assumed that higher level layers learn semantic structures whereas lower level layers learn scanner-specific textures, intensities. Therefore, only adapting to the lower level layers to reduce domain shift seemed better instinctively. Similarly, under image alignment, a study proposed CycleGAN for image synthesis, where an MR image is first mapped to the target domain and then mapped back to the original domain to measure the loss between the input and reconstructed image.

Similarly, multiple studies have been proposed over the years under different strategies. These strategies are then evaluated using corresponding benchmarks. A wide range of benchmarks has been explored, including brain, heart, eye, and lung datasets, for different purposes such as lung disease classification, tumor segmentation, and many more. However, major bottlenecks include limited training data, high-dimensional data, etc. Although pre-trained large-scale models can be used, they cannot easily adapt to medical images. Because of this lack of data, most research tends to focus more on unsupervised DA methods. Therefore, this remains a promising direction for future research.
