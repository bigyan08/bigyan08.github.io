\nIn this era of Artificial Intelligence, LLMs have dominated a wide range of areas, including medical and educational fields. They have demonstrated strong performance across various tasks like natural language understanding and generation. LLMs are generally pretrained using large-scale unlabeled datasets, which helps them capture general domain knowledge, but they still require instruction tuning or finetuning for real-world tasks. This gave rise to multimodal contexts, introducing VLMs. Therefore, recent research has been more prioritized towards contrastive learning (where a pair of image and text is provided, which pulls correct pairs together and pushes wrong pairs apart in embedding space). But this has a major issue: the lack of high-quality datasets at such a large scale, due to various reasons. So the researchers of this paper focused on building a foundation model trained solely on large-scale CXR image data, which they hypothesized would enable the model to achieve robust performance on downstream tasks. Work on DINO and DINOv2 on natural images inspired a similar self-supervised framework to train a ViT on CXR datasets.

The work builds on top of RAD-DINO, which used DINOv2 for pretraining models on CXRs, extending it by conducting a systematic empirical study of the key individual components within the DINOv2 training pipeline and evaluating their significance. It also tries to achieve improved performance even with smaller-sized models. Hence, it is divided into two models: CXformer(S) and CXformer(B). Main contributions include performing an empirical study on continual pretraining of DINOv2 on CXRs, simplifying the DINOv2 training pipeline, using that simplified pipeline to achieve comparable performance to the SOTA model with less compute, and finally releasing both models on HuggingFace to support reproducible research in this area. For the models, Vision Transformers are used, which show good performance on medical imaging tasks. The evaluation is done on CXR datasets like CheXpert, MIMIC-CXR, PadChest, NIH-CXR8, and BRAX. These datasets are diversified across different geographical regions like Brazil, USA, and Spain, different patient demographics like age groups, and clinical settings, along with some filtering. This will help the model with robustness and generalizing better.

The experimental setup is divided into two parts: Pretraining and Finetuning. First, the pretraining is continued by initializing both the student and teacher networks from a pretrained DINOv2 checkpoint. Registers are incorporated, which introduces learnable tokens to the ViT. Registers act as global memory, absorbing redundant information from low-informative patches and mitigating outliers. Studies show that adding registers improves performance with little to no computational overhead. Next is the finetuning portion. Here, the final teacher checkpoint is used as the backbone, and a task-specific head is attached. As an example, for the classification task on CheXpert, the head consists of a linear layer with five outputs. For evaluation, both techniques (linear probing and full finetuning) are used. The metric used for evaluation is AUROC for the image classification task, which evaluates the classifier's ability to differentiate between classes by measuring the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. For semantic segmentation, the Dice coefficient is used, and for report generation, ROUGE-L is used. The training details include: pretraining for 100 epochs utilizing 16 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, with a global batch size of 1024. The learning rate scheduler starts at 3e-4, with a warmup phase during the first 10% of training iterations. Some augmentations are included, like rotation, translation, scaling, color jittering, Gaussian blurring, and solarization.

The baseline DINOv2 model achieves an AUROC of 78%, and when continually pretrained on 10% of the CXR dataset, the ViT-S default model achieves an AUROC of 80%. This is obtained from the default DINOv2 setup, including Sinkhorn-Knopp centering, no registers, etc. Introducing registers further improved AUROC by 2%. The results also show that CXformer achieves comparable performance with almost 4x fewer parameters. The final training pipeline uses a ViT with registers, 16K prototype heads, and moving average centering, along with freezing of the backbone for the first epoch. For the image classification task, CXformer(B) outperforms the others, and it requires 46% less pretraining compute and 28% fewer data samples than RAD-DINO. The smaller model, CXformer(S), achieves a slightly lower AUROC but with significantly reduced pretraining compute. For the segmentation task, RAD-DINO achieves the highest overall performance of 92.3, whereas the CXformer models perform competitively, with a score of around 90 for both. Finally, for the radiology report generation task, both CXformer variants deliver competitive results across datasets, with CXformer(S) outperforming every model except on CheXbert-F1 score, while requiring 3 times fewer parameters, 7.4 times less pretraining compute, and 30% fewer training samples.

While the optimizations shown for adapting DINOv2 to CXR are promising, their effectiveness in other medical imaging modalities like CT/MRI is still uncertain, and this is an interesting direction for future research and study.
