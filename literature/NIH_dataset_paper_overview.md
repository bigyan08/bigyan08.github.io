\n## High-Level Summary of NIH dataset

The work on disease detection using computer vision is a highly studied topic. For the disease detection task, previous methods used a very small number of labels and were tested on only hundreds of patients. However, creating a proper automated system that classifies such diseases using CNNs requires a large dataset from tens of thousands of patients, since deep CNNs are data-hungry and require a large amount of data to produce good results. This is the problem that this paper is trying to overcome.

This paper mentions some major factors that make it difficult to scale medical image-based diagnosis. One is that labeling thousands and thousands of datasets is not feasible for experts and also cannot be crowdsourced to the public like ImageNet. Hence, NLP techniques were used on radiology reports to extract labels for the dataset. Another challenge is that the spatial dimensions of chest X-rays are usually very large, while the disease signal in the image can be extremely small. This creates problems in two ways: the large spatial dimensions are computationally expensive, and due to pooling and other feature extraction operations, the actual region that contains disease-related information may be phased away. To address this problem, the paper proposes and validates a weakly supervised multi-label image classification and disease localization framework.There were initially eight labels (which were later increased to fourteen), and the labels were identified based on the words present in the radiology reports. This entire approach forms a unified weakly supervised multi-label image classification and pathology localization framework. The newly created dataset introduced in this paper is significantly larger than previous datasets.

For validation, the OpenI dataset was chosen as the baseline for the eight specific diseases. On this dataset, the proposed method shows improvements in all metrics (precision, recall, and F1-score) compared to the previous method, MetaMap. The paper also mentions that using NLP techniques for negation and uncertainty detection successfully reduces false-positive cases. Additionally, for the disease localization task, a small subset of hand-drawn bounding box annotations was used.For this task, four CNN-based models pretrained on the ImageNet dataset were used. These models were then fully fine-tuned on the medical dataset after replacing their final output and fully connected layers with transition, pooling, and prediction layers. The training procedure involved CNN fine-tuning using Stochastic Gradient Descent with data splits of 70/10/20. Among the four models, the ResNet-50-based model produced the best results.

The results varied considerably across diseases. Some diseases achieved very good results, while others showed relatively lower performance due to high within-class variation. A few diseases performed poorly because they represented only a small portion of the overall dataset.Although this paper demonstrates significant improvements compared to previous research, this domain still contains many challenges that can be addressed in future work. As already mentioned, while the model may perform well on some diseases, diseases that frequently occur in combination with others tend to show poorer results. Furthermore, although the NLP technique demonstrated improvements over the previous MetaMap approach on the OpenI dataset, inconsistencies may still arise due to the way findings are described and phrased in radiology reports.
