\nIn medical image representation learning, much recent progress has been made, but it is constrained by the lack of data availability and the reporting bias of clinical narratives. Vision-language modeling (learning the relationship between an image and the text describing it) is explored mostly, but the authors are trying to find whether the text should really determine the visual information that an image encoder learns. Since the representations are reused during downstream tasks like classification, segmentation, etc., they remain fixed or frozen. This process mainly assumes that the textual information is sufficient to represent the visual information, but the reporting task is mainly focused on clinical findings and not exactly the "full image description." So, this can cause the image representation to be incomplete. Similarly, the visual encoders that are trained to align with text show some inherent bias and hence find the easiest solution instead of preserving all features. So, the better method would be to learn invariances, meaning the representation should remain similar even though there are some irrelevant visual changes like exposure or brightness. Self-distillation is a paradigm where a model uses its own past outputs to train itself. This focuses on invariance instead of context-to-target prediction. This way, it doesn't try to predict the pixels of missing patches but instead predicts their latent representation (which is a lower-dimensional encoding of raw data that captures its essential features and underlying patterns). The predicted representation tries to match the target representation. JEPA formalizes this by predicting abstract latent representations of masked regions. However, it is relatively unexplored compared to other methods.

RadJEPA is a self-supervised architecture for radiology representation learning of chest X-rays, which is motivated to overcome the limitations of language-supervised and view-alignment-based methods in order to learn the latent representation of X-ray images trained on 840k data. The authors have done extensive empirical evaluations of RadJEPA on report generation, where it is compared with image-only and vision-language baselines using a frozen Vicuna-7B decoder with different VLMs by replacing their encoders. It is also benchmarked on disease classification and semantic segmentation.

Chest X-ray representation learning methods can be broadly divided into image-text alignment and image-only self-supervised approaches. Image-text methods include CLIP, BioViL, etc., whereas image-only self-supervised methods include DINO, RAD-DINO, and RadJEPA. Although image-text methods show strong downstream performance, they have several limitations, as they heavily rely on paired radiology reports. Due to this, there could be reporting bias and inconsistent terminologies. The RadJEPA pretraining operates by sampling two non-overlapping regions, which are the context region 'c' and target region 't'. The visual encoder 'f' extracts the latent representation, after which a predictor 'g' estimates the target embeddings using a latent prediction objective. After pretraining, the encoder 'f' is frozen and reused for downstream tasks. For the disease classification task, the image is passed to a linear classifier, which produces probabilities optimized using cross-entropy, and only the classification head is trained. Similarly, for the semantic segmentation task, features are extracted and aggregated to produce pixel-wise logits using a segmentation loss. Here, only the decoder parameters are optimized. For the report generation task, the frozen visual embeddings are projected through a two-layer MLP adapter, where GeLU non-linearity is used. Then, the projected embeddings are concatenated with the instruction prompt and passed to the language model, which generates reports and is optimized via negative log-likelihood.

The evaluation is also done on classification and segmentation tasks. While these do not generally show the significance of computational linguistics, this is done to find the generalization capability of RadJEPA on foundational tasks. The report generation is evaluated on MIMIC-CXR and IU-Xray, where metrics like ROUGE-L and BLEU-4 were used. RadJEPA as an encoder (with a fixed decoder) achieves the best performance across all metrics and datasets in both the image-text paired and self-distillation settings. The authors also measured how much different each encoder learns compared to the base encoder. There are some cases where the model predicts the absence of pleural effusion and pneumothorax despite positive findings in the ground-truth report. RadJEPA, pretrained on around 840k unlabeled images, exceeded the strongest image-only and vision-language baselines across four VLM backbones, two datasets, and four metrics, and showed consistent improvement in classification and segmentation.

There are some limitations seen in this paper. The open-source datasets are mostly collected from specific hospitals and clinical environments and therefore introduce biases that may not generalize well across institutions. Also, the benchmarking datasets are relatively small, making them noisy and imperfect. Another limitation is representation collapse, where different types of chest X-ray images map to the same vector, learning nothing useful. There is no prevention for it at the objective level since the objective doesn't contain a term to define different embeddings for different images.
